# Advanced AI Assistants

## Definition

**Advanced AI Assistants** are AI applications, powered by foundation models, that primarily use a natural-language interface and are designed to play a particular role in relation to a user. The term was formally defined by the **Ada Lovelace Institute** in their February 2025 policy briefing *Delegation Nation*.

## Three Roles

Advanced AI Assistants are designed to take on one or more of three roles:

1. **Executor** — acting directly on the world on behalf of a user (e.g., OpenAI's Operator, which can browse the web and complete multi-step tasks; Anthropic's Claude Computer Use)
2. **Adviser** — instructing a user on how to accomplish a task or realise an objective (e.g., DoNotPay, a legal instruction bot)
3. **Interlocutor** — interacting with a user to bring about a particular mental state (e.g., Wysa, a mental health app)

## Key Technical Features

Advanced AI Assistants are distinguished from earlier virtual assistants by several technical capabilities:

- **Natural-language interface** — primary communication mode is text or speech
- **Personalisation** — capable of learning and incorporating user preferences over time
- **Context window elongation** — can store information about previous interactions for months or years
- **Tool use** — access to external tools (calculators, calendars, web browsers, email)
- **Chain-of-thought reasoning** — ability to break complex problems into smaller steps
- **Fine tuning** — further trained on specialized data or via RLHF/RLAIF
- **RAG (Retrieval Augmented Generation)** — queries databases for additional information

## How They Differ from Virtual Assistants

| Feature | Virtual Assistants | Advanced AI Assistants |
|---------|-------------------|----------------------|
| Reasoning | Rule-based (explicit rules) | Probabilistic (inferred patterns) |
| Transparency | Transparent and predictable | Opaque and unpredictable |
| Human control | Formalisations of human intention | Reflections of patterns in data |
| Flexibility | Brittle | Flexible |
| Versatility | Narrowly deployable | Versatile |

## Consumer Harms and Risks

The rise of Advanced AI Assistants poses multiple categories of risk to consumers:

### Technical risks
- **Reliability** — severe consequences if malfunctioning when heavily integrated
- **Accuracy** — bias and hallucination, harder to detect in personalized systems
- **Explainability** — probabilistic nature makes outputs harder to understand
- **Alignment** — high flexibility + broad deployment = difficult alignment with human values
- **Emergent behaviour** — unpredictable patterns between interacting AI Assistants

### Non-technical, non-systemic risks
- **Psychological impact** — potential for emotional dependency, substitution for human relationships
- **Practical impact** — undermining incentives to learn and practice skills
- **Nefarious uses** — automation of scams, easier access to dangerous information

### Non-technical, systemic risks
- **Market concentration** — power in hands of few large tech companies
- **Gatekeeping** — potential to become the primary interface for internet access
- **Privacy** — extensive data collection, users sharing more sensitive information
- **Delegation of decision-making** — significant rise in decisions delegated to AI with discretion
- **Distributional effects** — could exacerbate economic inequality
- **Labour displacement** — benefits may accrue predominantly to AI providers
