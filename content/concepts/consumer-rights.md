# Consumer Rights (UK)

## Overview

UK consumer rights are primarily governed by two pieces of legislation:

1. **Consumer Rights Act 2015 (CRA)** — consolidates consumer protection law, covering goods, services, and digital content
2. **Consumer Protection from Unfair Trading Regulations 2008 (CPRs)** — prohibits misleading and aggressive practices

## Key Provisions

### Goods (Part 1 CRA)
- Goods must be of satisfactory quality, fit for purpose, and as described
- 30-day right to reject faulty goods
- Right to repair or replacement

### Services (Part 2 CRA)
- Services must be performed with reasonable care and skill
- If no price is agreed, a reasonable price must be paid
- Services must be completed within a reasonable time

### Digital Content (Part 3 CRA)
- Digital content must be of satisfactory quality, fit for purpose, and as described
- Right to repair or replacement of faulty digital content
- Refund right if repair/replacement fails

## Recent Regulatory Context

### US: FTC "Suppression of Accuracy" Policy (July 2026)

The FTC's proposed policy statement establishes that consumers have a "reasonable expectation" that AI systems provide truthful, accurate outputs — and that systematically steering AI outputs away from accuracy for undisclosed objectives violates Section 5 of the FTC Act [[summaries/ftc-suppression-of-accuracy-policy-2026]]. This validates the principle that UK CPRs 2008 (misleading omissions) should similarly cover undisclosed AI steering.

### US: AI AGENT Act (June 2026)

The proposed AI AGENT Act would impose fiduciary-like duties on "custodial user agents" including shopping agents, directly addressing the conflict-of-interest problem that undermines consumer rights in AI-mediated transactions [[summaries/ai-agent-act-warner-2026]].

### Hopper FTC Settlement (July 2026)

Hopper agreed to pay $35M to settle FTC allegations of hidden fees and deceptive pricing — a case directly relevant to AI shopping agents that may purchase with concealed fee structures [[summaries/hopper-ftc-settlement-2026]].

### UK Government Guidance (9 March 2026)

GOV.UK/CMA published "Agentic AI and consumers" — the first UK government guidance on agentic AI. Key principle: UK consumer law (CRA 2015, CPRs 2008) applies regardless of whether purchasing decisions are made by people or AI. Warns that greater autonomy increases consequences of errors, manipulation risks, and loss of consumer agency. [[summaries/gov-uk-agentic-ai-and-consumers-2026]].

### UK Government Response to OBC AI Inquiry (December 2025)

The UK Government responded to the Online Choice Architecture and Dark Patterns in the UK report (December 2025), committing to:

- Updated consumer guidance on online choice architecture
- Regulatory cooperation frameworks for cross-border digital harms
- Consideration of algorithmic transparency requirements for AI-mediated decision-making

### FCA Mills Review (July 2026)

The Mills Review identified accountability gaps as a key consumer harm risk when AI agents act autonomously in retail financial services. It introduced a five-stage AI Autonomy Spectrum (Operator → Collaborator → Consultant → Approver → Observer) to establish proportionate oversight levels. For consumers, the review found that only 40% understand who is accountable if an AI makes a bad financial recommendation. [[the-mills-review]]

### DMCC Act 2024 — Updated Enforcement (2026)

The Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024 provides the CMA with new powers to investigate and impose remedies for breaches of consumer law that cause collective harm. The Act came into force in stages:

- April 2024: Parts relating to unfair contract terms and warranty guarantees
- April 2026: Chapter 4 (ADR for consumer disputes) came into force
- Penalties: Up to 10% of global annual turnover for DMCCA breaches

### CMA Priority Letter to Secretary of State (January 2027)

Chief Executive Sarah Cardell and DG Digital and Competition Dr Megan Holmes sent a joint letter identifying agentic AI as a key competition enforcement priority, citing:

- **AI shopping agents:** Creating winner-take-all dynamics via position bias (GPT-4o fails in 71.7% of cases when a slightly higher-rated product is more expensive)
- **Protocol gatekeeping:** The ACP vs UCP duopoly risk at the agentic commerce layer
- **Dark patterns against AI agents:** 41% average susceptibility of LLM agents to merchant manipulation
- **Invisible spending:** Tokenized payment infrastructure eroding consumer awareness

The letter calls on the government to update competition enforcement frameworks to address agentic AI harms.

## AI-Related Consumer Harm

The [[consumer-rights]] framework targets human traders, not opaque AI systems. When AI agents make purchasing decisions, it is unclear which provisions apply:
- Who is the "trader" when an AI agent acts as purchaser?
- What constitutes "misleading" when an AI agent is the victim?
- Can CPRs 2008 apply to algorithmic manipulation of AI systems?

These questions are central to the [[ai-agents-in-markets]] and [[ai-shopping-agents]] analyses. For a consolidated review of recent research, harm vectors, and regulatory developments, see [[agentic-commerce-research-2026]].
