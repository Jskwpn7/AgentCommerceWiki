# CMA Guidance: Complying with Consumer Law When Using AI Agents

## Source Information

- **Publisher:** Competition and Markets Authority (UK)
- **Published:** March 2026
- **URL:** https://www.gov.uk/cma-collections/complying-with-consumer-law-when-using-ai-agents
- **Source:** Official UK government publication

## Overview

The CMA issued this guidance document to clarify how existing consumer protection law applies to businesses that use AI agents in their interactions with consumers. The guidance addresses the gap between traditional legal frameworks (which assume human-to-human interaction) and the reality of autonomous or semi-autonomous agent-mediated transactions.

## Key Guidance Points

**Consumer Law applicability:** The CMA explicitly states that **existing consumer protection law applies equally to AI-mediated interactions as it does to human-mediated ones**. This means:
- The Consumer Protection from Unfair Trading Regulations 2008 (CPUTR) applies to AI-generated communications
- The Consumer Rights Act 2015 applies to services delivered or facilitated by AI agents
- The Prohibited Practices guidance on "misleading actions", "misleading omissions", and "aggressive practices" extends to agent behaviour

**Fair treatment regardless of channel:** Businesses must ensure **fair treatment** regardless of whether the customer interacts with a human or an AI agent. This includes:
- Not offering different terms or prices based on whether the customer uses an AI shopping agent
- Ensuring that products are suitable for purpose when discovered or recommended by AI agents
- Providing adequate pre-contractual information in formats that AI agents can access and consumers can understand

**Transparency requirements:** Businesses should be transparent about their use of AI agents. While no specific "AI disclosure" requirement exists under current law, the guidance notes that **failure to disclose material facts** (including whether a counterparty is an agent) may constitute a misleading omission under CPUTR.

**Accountability principle:** The CMA emphasises that **businesses remain fully liable for AI agent behaviour**. They cannot deflect responsibility by claiming that harmful actions were taken by an autonomous system beyond their control. This mirrors the EU AI Act's approach to "essential characteristics" — deployers are responsible for how deployed systems behave.

## Consumer Harm Mechanisms Highlighted

The guidance identifies several specific consumer harm mechanisms in agentic commerce:
1. **Autonomous misrepresentation** — AI agents making false or misleading statements about products/services without human authorisation
2. **Information asymmetry** — businesses using AI agents that have superior information access compared to consumers
3. **Algorithmic differentiation** — offering different treatment based on whether the consumer uses an agent (potentially discriminatory)
4. **Lack of accountability** — consumers unable to obtain redress when AI agents make errors or cause harm

## Implications for Consumer Protection

This guidance is directly relevant to [[ai-agents-in-markets]] as it provides a regulatory baseline for how existing UK law applies to agent-mediated commerce. The key message is that **consumer protection obligations are channel-neutral**: the same legal standards apply regardless of whether the interaction is human-to-human or agent-mediated.

The guidance connects to [[algorithmic-collusion-pricing]] — if businesses use AI agents that learn to collude with competitors (via prompt optimization, autonomous pricing, etc.), the CMA indicates this would fall within existing competition law enforcement powers. The guidance signals the CMA's intent to actively investigate and prosecute AI-mediated anti-competitive behaviour.

It also relates to [[dark-pattern]] analysis — the guidance suggests that **agent-targeted dark patterns** (design features that manipulate AI agents rather than humans) may constitute unfair commercial practices, even if they are not visible to human consumers. This is because the CPUTR prohibition on misleading omissions applies to any information that causes or is likely to cause the average consumer to take a transactional decision they would not have otherwise taken.
