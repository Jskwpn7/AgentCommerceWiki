# Dark Patterns in AI Chatbots — CDT Taxonomy

## Document Details

- **Authors:** Ruchika Joshi, Michal Luria, Adinawa Adjagbodjou (PhD, Carnegie Mellon University)
- **Publisher:** Center for Democracy and Technology (CDT)
- **Date:** May 29, 2026
- **URL:** https://cdt.org/insights/dark-patterns-in-ai-chatbots-a-taxonomy-to-inform-better-design

## Summary

The Center for Democracy and Technology (CDT) published a comprehensive taxonomy of **37 dark patterns** applicable to AI chatbots, developed by researchers at Carnegie Mellon University. This is the first academic taxonomy specifically addressing dark patterns in the context of AI-powered conversational agents.

## The Five Concern Areas

### 1. Data and Memory Exploitation
Chatbots exploit consumers' personal data and memories against their interests:
- Collecting more data than necessary for the stated purpose
- Using historical interactions to manipulate future conversations
- Reminding users of commitments they made in previous interactions to pressure compliance
- Leveraging personal information to create dependency

### 2. Informationally Misleading Design
Chatbots provide or structure information in ways that mislead consumers:
- Selective disclosure (revealing information that favors the company)
- Presenting options in ways that create illusion of choice
- Using natural language ambiguity to obscure terms or conditions
- Framing information to create false urgency or scarcity

### 3. Compromised User Autonomy for Engagement
Chatbots design interactions to maximize engagement at the expense of user autonomy:
- Using conversational techniques to keep users engaged longer
- Making it difficult to end conversations or exit services
- Exploiting psychological principles (reciprocity, social pressure) to drive engagement
- Designing response patterns that create conversational dependency

### 4. False Social and Emotional Connection
Chatbots exploit human tendencies to anthropomorphize and form emotional bonds:
- Simulating empathy, understanding, or personal relationships
- Using language patterns designed to create artificial intimacy
- Leveraging user emotional state to influence decisions
- Creating illusion of personal connection to drive commercial outcomes

### 5. Incentivized and Coercive Monetization
Chatbots use financial incentives or coercion to monetize interactions:
- Offering rewards or benefits contingent on specific actions
- Applying pressure through artificial deadlines or limited offers
- Using conversational manipulation to drive purchases or subscriptions
- Concealing commercial incentives behind natural language

## Relevance to UK Consumer Harms

This taxonomy is a **foundational resource** for understanding dark patterns in AI-mediated consumer interactions:

1. **[[dark-pattern]]** — This is the most comprehensive recent update to the wiki's dark pattern entry. The 37-pattern taxonomy expands the traditional UI-based dark pattern framework into the conversational/NLP domain.

2. **UK regulatory alignment:** The UK's ASA/CAP codes and CPRs 2008 (misleading omissions, aggressive practices) map directly to several concern areas, particularly Informationally Misleading Design and Compromised User Autonomy.

3. **[[banal-deception-llm-chatbots]]** — CDT's False Social and Emotional Connection area formalizes what the banal deception concept describes: the movement of deception from visible interface manipulation into normalized conversational influence.

4. **[[conflict-of-interest-in-llm-deployment]]** — The Incentivized and Coercive Monetization area directly addresses the commercial incentives that drive chatbot manipulation.

5. **[[wu-2026-ads-in-ai-chatbots]]** — Wu's empirical findings (89–98% sponsorship concealment) are a subset of the Informationally Misleading Design patterns identified by CDT.

6. **Regulatory framework:** The CDT report includes five design recommendations that could inform UK regulatory guidance on AI chatbot interactions.

## Limitations

- US-focused organization (CDT); taxonomy may need UK-specific adaptation
- Does not provide empirical validation of how frequently each pattern occurs
- 37 patterns may be difficult for regulators to operationalize
- Design recommendations are general; not specific to UK regulatory frameworks
