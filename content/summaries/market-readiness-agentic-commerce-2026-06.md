# Market Readiness and Trust in Agentic Commerce

**Date:** 2026-06-30
**Sources:** MIT IDE, Checkout.com

## Executive Summary

MIT IDE researchers and Checkout.com's global survey both point to a fundamental asymmetry: demand for AI-mediated shopping is forming rapidly while trust and infrastructure lag significantly behind. The gap between consumer expectations and merchant/regulatory readiness creates systematic consumer harm risk.

## The Demand Readiness Gap

| Metric | Source | Value |
|---|---|---|
| Consumers expecting 10%+ AI-driven purchases within a year | Checkout.com survey | 33% |
| Consumers who will never delegate to AI | Checkout.com survey | 24% |
| Consumers trusting no organization with AI agent | Checkout.com survey | 27% |
| Merchants saying consumers will adopt faster than businesses ready | Checkout.com survey | 72% |
| Merchants already testing agentic commerce | Checkout.com survey | 42% |

The 72% figure is critical: **nearly three-quarters of merchants believe adoption will outpace readiness.** This creates a period of regulatory and infrastructural vulnerability where consumers may be exposed to AI-mediated harms without adequate protections.

## The Transaction Cost Disruption Thesis

MIT IDE's core argument: AI shopping will **lower transaction costs to almost nothing**, fundamentally challenging the economic rationale for human intermediaries.

Traditional intermediaries exist because they are:
1. Cheaper than consumers doing it themselves
2. More expert than average consumers
3. More experienced at the transaction

AI agents threaten all three rationales — creating market disruption but also raising questions about whose interests agents optimize.

## Consumer Trust Requirements

For consumers to delegate spending to AI agents, they demand:
- **Spending limits** must be settable
- **Purchase categories** must be configurable
- **Cancellation** must be easy and immediate
- **Transparency** about what the agent is doing

The absence of these controls in current agent implementations represents a significant consumer harm vector.

## Market Structure Implications

MIT IDE research suggests:
- Traditional e-commerce models (product pages, search, recommendation engines) may become obsolete
- Businesses must make product data **agent-readable** (structured data, APIs)
- Platforms that agents use become **new gatekeepers**
- **Competition risk:** Agents may favor merchants with best agent-optimization, not best products

## Connection to UK Consumer Harms

| Vector | Description |
|---|---|
| **Gatekeeper power** | New intermediary layer between consumers and products creates concentration risk |
| **Preference distortion** | Agents may optimize for merchant interests rather than consumer welfare |
| **Trust deficit** | 27% trust no organization — the largest single trust barrier |
| **Regulatory lag** | 72% of merchants see adoption outpacing readiness — regulatory frameworks are falling behind |
| **Market disruption** | If intermediaries collapse without adequate replacement frameworks, consumers lose expertise and protection |

## Comparison with Fortune Brainstorm Tech

Both MIT IDE and Fortune Brainstorm Tech (June 12, 2026) converge on the finding that "no one is ready" — the MIT analysis provides the economic framework while Fortune Brainstorm Tech documents the industry awareness gap.
