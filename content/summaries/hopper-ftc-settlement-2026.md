# Hopper FTC Settlement ($35M) — Hidden Fees and Deceptive Practices

## Document Details

- **Publisher:** Federal Trade Commission (FTC)
- **Type:** Settlement agreement
- **Date:** July 2, 2026
- **Amount:** $35 million consumer settlement
- **URL:** https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/07/travel-app-hopper-pay-35-million-settle-ftc-allegations-it-charged-fees-without-consent-deceived

## Summary

Travel-booking app **Hopper** agreed to pay **$35 million** to settle FTC allegations that it charged consumers hidden fees without meaningful consent and misled users about the total prices they would pay and the benefits of its paid VIP Support and Price Freeze services.

## Allegations

The FTC's complaint alleged that Hopper:

1. **Pre-selected and hid fees:** The "Tip" and "VIP Support" fees were pre-selected on a booking screen that consumers had to scroll to see, meaning they were not affirmatively consented to.

2. **Misrepresented "no hidden fees" marketing:** Hopper marketed itself as having "no hidden fees" while simultaneously charging concealed fees through its booking flow design.

3. **Deceived about product benefits:** The company misrepresented the actual benefits and value of its VIP Support and Price Freeze paid services.

## Settlement Terms

- $35 million consumer settlement
- Prohibited from deceiving consumers about fees
- Required to clearly disclose the total price of goods and services
- Required to disclose fees and charges before completing transactions
- Required to disclose the final amount consumers will pay before completing a transaction

## Relevance to UK Consumer Harms

This is a **direct parallel** to UK consumer protection concerns:

1. **[[dark-pattern]]** — Hopper's pre-selected fees on a scrollable screen is a textbook dark pattern (specifically, a "hidden cost" variant). The FTC treated this as deceptive practice under Section 5.

2. **UK CPRs 2008:** The UK's Consumer Protection from Unfair Trading Regulations 2008 prohibit misleading actions and misleading omissions. Hopper's practices would likely violate both:
   - **Misleading action:** Presenting pre-selected fees as optional tips
   - **Misleading omission:** Failing to disclose the total price until late in the transaction

3. **FTC "junk fee" crackdown:** This case is part of a broader regulatory throughline targeting hidden fees across travel, ticketing, lodging, and auto sales. UK regulators (CMA, FCA) have expressed similar concerns about junk fees.

4. **AI shopping agent implications:** As AI shopping agents enter the market, consumers may be even more exposed to hidden fees — AI agents could be steered toward bookings with hidden fees, and consumers would have even less visibility into the total cost structure than they had with Hopper.

5. **[[wu-2026-ads-in-ai-chatbots]]** — The FTC's approach to undisclosed fees parallels the sponsorship concealment problem: in both cases, consumers are denied material information that affects purchasing decisions.
