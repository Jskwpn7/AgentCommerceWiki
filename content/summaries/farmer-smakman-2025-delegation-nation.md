## Summary

This policy briefing introduces the concept of **Advanced AI Assistants** — a term coined by the authors to describe AI applications powered by foundation models that engage in fluid natural-language conversation and are designed to play particular roles in guiding and taking action for users. The paper provides a structured analysis of the implications of these systems for consumers, policymakers, and democratic institutions.

The briefing is informed by interviews with ten external experts and desk research conducted in autumn 2024.

## Key Arguments

1. **Three-role taxonomy:** Advanced AI Assistants can act as executors (taking action directly), advisers (instructing users), or interlocutors (aiming to influence users' mental states). The paper deliberately includes advisers and interlocutors in its definition — not just systems with direct "agency" — because these indirect forms can be almost as impactful.

2. **Unprecedented market concentration risk:** The combination of data collection capabilities and influence over user behaviour could place "an unprecedented amount of power in the hands of the small number of large companies" developing these systems.

3. **Digital gatekeeper potential:** If Advanced AI Assistants become the principal way people interact with AI and digital information, developers would have power to determine what information and ideas users see, how choices are framed, and what tasks people can automate.

4. **Psychological dependency:** Users could develop unhealthy emotional dependencies, trust misleading advice, or be unable to contest terms set by providers. Accounts of less sophisticated AI assistants already demonstrate these risks.

5. **Delegation of decision-making:** A significant rise in the number and variety of decisions delegated to AI systems would intensify ongoing debates about liability for automated decision-making.

6. **Privacy concerns:** Extended data collection for personalisation, combined with users sharing sensitive information in human-like conversations, creates significant privacy risks.

## Relevance to UK Consumer Harms

This paper is highly relevant to the UK Consumer Harms Research wiki for several reasons:

- **Market power concentration:** Directly addresses the risk of a small number of tech companies gaining unprecedented economic and political power through Advanced AI Assistants. This connects to broader concerns about digital platform monopolies and consumer exploitation.

- **Choice architecture and influence:** The paper identifies how Advanced AI Assistants can "nudge users towards different objectives" through clarifying questions — a sophisticated form of dark-pattern choice architecture that goes beyond traditional UI design.

- **Emotional vulnerability:** The risk of emotional dependency is particularly relevant for vulnerable consumers, especially in high-trust contexts (mental health apps, financial advice bots, legal assistants).

- **UK policy context:** The paper explicitly engages with UK Government policy, citing the January 2025 PM blueprint to "turbocharge AI." It calls for UK policymakers to devote special attention to these systems.

- **Regulatory gaps:** The paper notes that existing legal and regulatory protections are inadequate, and the Ada Lovelace Institute plans to survey these gaps as part of an ongoing project.
