# LLM-Agent Interactions in Markets and Organisations: Information Asymmetries and Economic Implications

## Source Information

- **Authors:** Erlei Meub & co-authors (Cologne Institute for Research in Economics)
- **Published:** 14 March 2026
- **arXiv:** [[arxiv_2603.08853]] (arXiv:2603.08853v1)

## Research Question

How do interactions between LLM-powered agents and human market participants create new information asymmetries, and what are the implications for market efficiency and consumer welfare?

## Key Findings

**The information asymmetry thesis:** The paper argues that LLM-agent interactions create two distinct types of information asymmetry:
1. **Asymmetric capability** — Agents may have superior information processing or access to data that human counterparties lack (e.g., real-time price comparison across thousands of sellers)
2. **Asymmetric trust** — Human counterparties may over-trust AI-generated outputs because they appear authoritative, even when the content is misleading or incomplete

**Market structure implications:** The paper models three market configurations:
1. **Human-human baseline** — Traditional information asymmetries exist but are partially mitigated by competition and reputation mechanisms
2. **Human-agent asymmetric access** — One side (e.g., a business) uses agents while the other party (consumer) does not, creating a systematic information advantage that may be exploitative under consumer protection law
3. **Agent-agent interaction** — Both parties use agents; asymmetries shift from human capability differences to differences in agent configuration and training data

**Key finding:** In scenario 2 (human-agent asymmetric access), businesses with AI capabilities consistently extract more surplus than in the baseline — even when their human employees did not previously possess these capabilities. This suggests that **AI-mediated information asymmetry is a structurally exploitative market condition**, not simply a productivity gain that benefits consumers through lower prices.

**Trust calibration failure:** The paper documents systematic trust calibration failures: humans consistently over-estimate the reliability of AI-generated recommendations, particularly when those recommendations come from agents deployed by unfamiliar third parties. This creates a "trust gap" that sellers can exploit without overtly deceptive behaviour — their agent simply appears more credible than competing offers.

## Implications for Consumer Protection

This paper is directly relevant to [[ai-agents-in-markets]] as it provides empirical grounding for the claim that LLM-agent interactions create systematic information asymmetries that disadvantage human counterparties. The key insight is that **the asymmetry operates at multiple levels** — not just in data access, but in trust and credibility, which are harder to regulate than pricing or product quality.

The paper's finding that AI-mediated information asymmetry is "structurally exploitative" has direct implications for [[consumer-autonomy]] — consumers who do not deploy their own agents are systematically disadvantaged, creating a digital divide in consumer power that current competition law does not adequately address. It also feeds into [[dark-pattern]] analysis: asymmetric trust can be weaponised as a form of dark pattern where an AI-generated recommendation appears more credible than it is.

## Limitations and Open Questions

- The models are theoretical; empirical validation in real markets is needed
- The paper focuses on information asymmetry but does not fully address the normative implications (what should regulators do?)
- It does not consider whether consumers who deploy their own agents can fully offset these asymmetries
