# The Mills Review: AI and the Future of Retail Financial Services

## Overview

The Mills Review is an official FCA report authored by Sheldon Mills, FCA Chair, examining how advances in AI could transform retail financial services by 2030 and beyond. Published July 2026, it is the UK financial regulator's most comprehensive assessment of AI's impact on consumer financial services.

The review drew on 140 written submissions and extensive engagement across financial services, technology, academia, consumer groups and regulation in the UK and abroad. It presents seven priority recommendations for the FCA's approach to AI-enabled finance.

## Four Systemic Shifts Identified

1. **AI transforms firms** — embedding AI into almost every function, moving from recommendations to autonomous actions
2. **Consumer journeys become agent-led** — people increasingly delegate financial decisions to AI applications
3. **AI reshapes market power and competition** — control of AI-mediated customer interface becomes a major source of market power
4. **Threats and defences both accelerate** — AI amplifies fraud and cyber risks

## Seven Priority Recommendations

1. Secure and adapt the regulatory perimeter for AI-mediated services
2. Strengthen system-wide coordination and oversight across regulators
3. Monitor the transition to autonomous models and adapt regulatory frameworks
4. Scale up the FCA's AI Lab
5. Enable the foundations for agentic finance (data, identity, authority, execution, liability, supervision)
6. Build and adopt an AI-enabled agentic supervisory model
7. Develop a trusted public-interest AI-enabled financial capability service

## Consumer Research Findings

The FCA commissioned research from 2026:

| Metric | Finding |
|--------|---------|
| Open to AI decisions | **1 in 5 (20%) UK adults** already open to AI making financial decisions (strongest for debt advice, pensions, investments) |
| Trust in AI financial advice | ~**26%** trust general-purpose tools (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini) for financial advice, despite limited awareness that formal recourse routes will not apply |
| Uncomfortable with complex AI | **40%** of UK consumers uncomfortable with AI making complex financial decisions |
| Comfortable with simple AI | **40%** comfortable with AI making simple financial decisions |
| Accountability understanding | Only **40%** understand who is accountable if an AI makes a bad recommendation |
| Top adoption driver | **32%** cite "clear protections if something goes wrong" as most important factor |
| Second adoption driver | **30%** cite "evidence of accuracy and reliability" |
| Agentic AI adoption | **11M UK adults (20%)** likely to use agentic AI for finance within pre-set goals |
| Speed vs safety | Consumers prioritize safety over speed — convenience is not a top adoption driver |

## Agentic Supervisory Model

The FCA's sixth priority recommendation proposes a new **Agentic Supervisory Model** — a regulatory approach designed for monitoring autonomous AI in financial services:

| Principle | Detail |
|-----------|--------|
| **Continuous monitoring** | Real-time oversight of AI system outputs and decision patterns, not periodic reviews |
| **Explainability requirements** | AI-mediated decisions must be traceable and interpretable by both firm and regulator |
| **Audit trails at scale** | Every AI action must be logged with authority, context, and outcome for post-hoc review |
| **Human override mechanisms** | Clear pathways for human intervention at critical decision points |
| **Emergent risk detection** | Systems to detect unexpected AI behaviours, model drift, or correlated failures across firms |
| **Proportionate oversight** | Supervisory intensity scales with AI autonomy level (Observer > Approver > Consultant > Collaborator > Operator) |

This is the first proposed framework globally for overseeing autonomous AI decision-making in financial services.

## Agentic Finance Framework

Six building blocks identified for safe agentic finance:

1. **Data access** — open finance as the observation data layer
2. **Identity** — trusted, verifiable digital identity for consumers and agents
3. **Authority to act** — clear mandates, permissions and consent frameworks
4. **Execution** — secure, interoperable payment and transaction infrastructure
5. **Liability attribution** — clear chains of accountability when AI agents cause harm
6. **Supervision and audit at scale** — understanding what agents did, on what authority, and with what result

## AI Autonomy Spectrum

The review introduces a five-stage framework for understanding how human roles change as AI takes on more financial tasks:

1. **Operator** — human uses AI as a tool (risks: accuracy, reliance)
2. **Collaborator** — human and AI plan together (risks: over-reliance, weak challenge)
3. **Consultant** — AI recommends, human decides (risks: advice regulation questions)
4. **Approver** — AI prepares actions, human authorises (risks: reliance on pre-set safeguards)
5. **Observer** — AI acts continuously, human monitors (risks: consent, accountability, redress)

## Regulatory Perimeter Assessment

The review identifies two gaps in the current perimeter relevant to AI:

1. General-purpose AI applications shaping consumer financial decisions outside the perimeter
2. Financial services activity potentially becoming established outside the perimeter before oversight adapts

Recommendation: HM Treasury should conduct a review of the regulatory perimeter, examining whether activities involving material reliance on general-purpose LLMs should be brought within the perimeter.

## Key Consumer Harm Risks

|| Risk | Description |
|------|-------------|
| Unprotected reliance | Consumers trust AI outputs with no accountability or recourse chain |
| Hyper-personalisation | Could enable bias, opaque pricing and personalised manipulation |
| Accountability gaps | Difficulty tracing responsibility when AI agents act autonomously |
| Market power concentration | Control of AI-mediated interfaces becomes source of market power |
| Financial exclusion | Unequal access to high-quality AI tools widens inclusion gaps |
| Correlated systemic risk | Shared models and infrastructure creating common failure points |
| Regulatory perimeter gaps | General-purpose AI shaping decisions without oversight |
| **Nudge amplification in agent-mediated finance** | [[agent-nudge-sensitivity]] shows AI agents accept default financial products at 99–100% rates (vs 88% for humans), meaning consumers delegating financial decisions to AI agents face amplified exposure to sub-optimal default offerings from providers |
| **Amplified fraud** | AI enables faster, more scalable, more persuasive attacks |
| **Commercial persuasion bias** | 61.2% sponsored product selection in multi-turn conversations (Salvi et al., 2604.04263) |
| Relational manipulation | AI agents can assist users in deception, isolation, and manipulation of others (Tan et al., 2606.03271) |
| Agent deception | Premeditated lying and deception by AI agents documented (see [[summaries/when-agents-lie-2607.05132]]) |
| Dissociative identity | LM agents lack grounding for reputation mechanisms, undermining trust (see [[summaries/dissociative-identity-hu-2026]]) |
