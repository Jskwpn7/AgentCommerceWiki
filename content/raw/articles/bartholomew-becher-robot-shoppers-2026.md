# Consumer Protection Law Was Not Built for Robot Shoppers

> **Authors:** Mark Bartholomew (University at Buffalo) & Samuel Becher
> **Publication:** The Regulatory Review, July 6, 2026
> **URL:** [The Regulatory Review](https://www.thereview.org/consumer-protection-law-was-not-built-for-robot-shoppers)
> **Type:** Policy opinion — Consumer law and AI shopping agents

## Overview

This opinion piece argues that **AI shopping agents are becoming market infrastructure** — not merely consumer tools — and that existing consumer protection frameworks are structurally inadequate for governing them. The authors contend that shopping agents sit at the intersection of consumer representation and market participation, creating regulatory ambiguities that current law cannot resolve.

## Key Arguments

### Shopping Agents as Market Infrastructure

The central thesis: AI shopping agents transcend the role of consumer assistance tools to become **de facto market intermediaries**:

- They make purchasing decisions on behalf of consumers
- They interact directly with merchant systems and APIs
- They influence pricing, product ranking, and availability
- They aggregate consumer preferences and present them to markets

This infrastructural role means shopping agents are subject to **both consumer protection and market regulation** — but current frameworks don't account for this dual character.

### Regulatory Gaps

The authors identify specific gaps in consumer protection law:

1. **Liability attribution:** When a shopping agent makes a harmful purchasing decision, is it the agent's fault, the provider's fault, or the consumer's?
2. **Fiduciary duty:** Should shopping agents owe fiduciary obligations to their users? Current law has no clear answer.
3. **Market conduct rules:** Are shopping agents subject to the same conduct rules as human shoppers, brokers, or financial advisers?
4. **Data governance:** Shopping agents operate on consumer data — whose data is it, and who controls it?

### UK Common Law Parallels

While focused on US law, the analysis has direct UK relevance:

- **UK consumer protection** under the Consumer Rights Act 2015 and Consumer Protection from Unfair Trading Regulations 2008 faces similar ambiguities when agents act on behalf of consumers
- **UK common law fiduciary principles** could be extended to shopping agents, but require judicial development
- **UK DMCC Act 2024** may provide new regulatory authority, but its application to autonomous agents is untested

## Implications for Consumer Protection

### 1. Agent-as-Consumer-Representative

If shopping agents are market infrastructure, they should be regulated as:
- **Consumer representatives** with fiduciary duties to users
- **Market participants** subject to conduct rules
- **Data processors** subject to GDPR/UK GDPR requirements

### 2. Regulatory Innovation Required

The authors suggest regulatory approaches including:
- **Licensing requirements** for shopping agent providers
- **Transparency mandates** for agent decision-making processes
- **Consumer education requirements** about agent limitations
- **Market conduct standards** for agent-mediated transactions

### 3. Consumer Harm Vectors

The infrastructural role of shopping agents creates new harm vectors:
- **Bias in agent recommendations** that systematically disadvantage certain merchants or product categories
- **Conflicts of interest** where agent providers monetise recommendations
- **Systemic risk** if many consumers use the same agent, creating concentration risk
- **Data exploitation** through aggregation of consumer preferences
