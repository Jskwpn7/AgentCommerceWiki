# AI Agents Want to Shop for You: The Future of Agentic Commerce

**Source:** MIT Initiative on the Digital Economy (IDE)
**Date:** June 3, 2026
**Authors:** Ben Manning, Peyman Shahidi (AI, Marketplaces and Labor Markets research group, Horton group)
**URL:** https://ide.mit.edu/insights/ai-agents-want-to-shop-for-you-the-future-of-agentic-commerce/

## Overview

This blog post by MIT IDE researchers theorizes that AI shopping will **lower transaction costs to almost nothing**, fundamentally challenging the economic rationale for human intermediaries in commerce.

## Key Arguments

### Transaction Cost Theory Applied to AI Shopping

The paper argues that human intermediaries exist for three reasons:
1. They're cheaper than consumers doing it themselves
2. They have expertise the consumer lacks
3. They've done that kind of transaction many more times

AI agents threaten all three rationales simultaneously. When transaction costs approach zero, the traditional role of intermediaries collapses.

### Implications for Market Structure

- **Demand side:** Consumers delegate increasingly complex purchasing decisions to AI agents
- **Supply side:** Businesses must adapt to being "seen" by agents, not just humans
- **Market design:** Traditional e-commerce models (product pages, search, recommendation engines) may become obsolete

### Case Example

The post cites Ben Manning's co-author's paper: someone could use an AI agent to search for and secure an apartment **instead of a human apartment broker**. This illustrates how even non-traditional e-commerce (real estate, services) is vulnerable to agent-mediated disruption.

## Connection to UK Consumer Harms

- **Market concentration risk:** If transaction costs collapse, only the largest platforms with sufficient data will remain competitive, increasing market concentration
- **Consumer dependency:** As agents become the primary shopping interface, consumers lose direct contact with product information
- **Regulatory challenge:** Current consumer law assumes human decision-making; agents operate differently
- **Cross-regulatory implications:** A single agent deployment could touch CMA (competition), ICO (data protection), FCA (financial services)
