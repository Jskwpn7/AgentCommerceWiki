# Is 2026 the Year of Agentic Payments?

**Authors:** Andrew Klungness, Julia Arruda Rosenthal, Andrea Louie, Igor Voloshin (Fenwick & West)
**Date:** 2026
**Source:** [Fenwick & West](https://www.fenwick.com/insights/publications/is-2026-the-year-of-agentic-payments)

## Summary

Comprehensive legal analysis from Fenwick & West examining the emerging landscape of agentic payments — transactions initiated, managed, and executed by adaptive AI systems with delegated authority. The article identifies three key legal/regulatory challenges: (1) AI laws and regulations (layered framework, state vs federal uncertainty), (2) money transmission licenses (whether platform control over payment flows triggers MSB/licensing requirements), and (3) consumer protection (EFTA/Regulation E's "authorized vs unauthorized" transfer distinction doesn't map cleanly to AI agents).

## Key Findings

**Definition:** Agentic payments are "payment transactions initiated, managed, and executed by adaptive AI systems that can act autonomously, with delegated authority to act on behalf of a user." This goes beyond traditional "autopay" because agents make decisions and take actions to achieve user-defined goals.

**Protocol landscape:** Several open payment protocols designed to solve agent interoperability and consent verification:
- **Agent Payments Protocol (AP2)** — led by Google
- **x402 extension on A2A** — developed by Coinbase (crypto-native model)
- **Machine Payments Protocol (MPP)** — co-authored by Stripe and Tempo
- **Mastercard and Visa** — announced intent to adopt agentic payments using existing authentication standards

**Crypto as "AI-native" currency:** Stablecoins and digital assets offer advantages for agentic payments: push-based (sender-initiated) transactions, frictionless cross-border settlement, 24/7 availability, ease of microtransactions, and code-layered spending limits.

**Three legal challenges:**

1. **AI Laws and Regulations** — Layered framework combining AI-specific and financial services laws; Trump admin released National Policy Framework for AI (March 2026) recommending federal framework to prevent state patchwork; general-purpose laws (product liability, consumer protection, agency relationships) untested on AI agents.

2. **Money Transmission Licenses** — Existing regime built around human intermediaries. The key question: whether the platform has retained "meaningful authority over payment flows." Agentic tools that merely execute pre-authorized transactions with no platform override are more likely to be deemed "pure technology conduits." Platforms that hold/wallet keys, maintain override capability, pause/redirect transactions based on their own risk logic, or pool funds are likely subject to heightened scrutiny and licensing.

3. **Consumer Protection (EFTA/Regulation E)** — The core distinction is "authorized vs unauthorized" transfer. Unresolved: does granting an AI agent access to bank accounts/payment credentials satisfy Regulation E's authorization requirements? Can an AI agent violate consumer instructions under current law? Regulation E not extended to crypto-native rails.

**Dispute rights gap:** No clear framework for handling disputes in agentic commerce. AP2 uses cryptographically signed "mandates" capturing user intent, but this code-based authorization is untested in courts.

## UK Consumer Harm Relevance

- **Regulatory gap identification:** The EFTA/Regulation E authorization gap maps directly to UK consumer protection concerns — if an AI agent makes an unauthorized purchase on behalf of a UK consumer, is it an "unauthorized EFT" under current law?
- **Liability allocation:** The article asks "who pays when the agents fail?" — user, developer, platform, or merchant? This is the core liability gap UK regulators need to address.
- **Money transmission licensing:** The platform-control vs pure-conduit distinction could inform how UK regulators view fintech platforms offering agentic payment features.
- **Code-based authorization:** The article notes AP2's cryptographically signed mandates are "untested in courts" — raises questions about enforceability of code-based consumer consent under UK law.
