# Agentic AI and Consumer Protection

CERRE | By Christoph Busch | 16 July 2026

## Content

**Core question:** Is EU consumer law ready for AI agents? Online shopping is entering a new phase. Consumers already use AI tools to discover products and compare offers, but the more fundamental change is agentic AI: systems that do not only recommend, but act. Some estimates suggest 10-20% of e-commerce transactions could be handled by an AI agent by 2030.

## Key Legal Challenge

The paper spells out an implicit assumption of EU consumer law: **that the person making the purchasing decision is a human being**. Once an AI agent sits between consumer and trader, this assumption no longer applies.

## Where Framework Comes Under Strain

- **Disclosure rules** built around limits of human attention — agents do not share these limits
- **Consent:** EU law requires consumers to explicitly acknowledge payment obligation; whether an agent can do so on consumer's behalf remains unresolved
- Many consumer protection requirements presuppose a human interacting via screen; as agents transact directly through APIs, compliance becomes structurally impossible

## Agentic-Specific Risks

1. **Merchant-operated agent incentive:** An agent operated by a merchant may have inherent incentive to steer purchasing decisions to its own products — preferencing may be opaque to consumer
2. **Agent manipulation:** Agents can be manipulated through "prompt injection" attacks; EU law protects consumers from dark patterns but offers no equivalent protection for agents acting on their behalf

## Liability Convergence Problem

When an agent buys wrong product or authorizes a payment it should not have, existing rules say little about how risk falls between consumer, agent provider and trader. Answer comes from neither consumer law alone — intersects with contract law, AI Act and product liability rules. Without clear liability architecture, consumers lack reason to trust delegated decision-making; providers lack incentive to offer it.

## Policy Directions

Three priorities for the Digital Fairness Act (expected Q3 2026):

1. **Settle foundations:** Clarify validity of contracts concluded through AI agents, explain how consumer law applies to agentic transactions
2. **Move from compliance by design to compliance by protocol:** Embed information duties, consent and redress into technical protocols — making compliance self-enforcing
3. **Differentiate and keep rules proportionate:** Agent requiring consumer confirmation ≠ autonomous agent; marketplace-embedded agent ≠ independent or OS-built agent. Rules should be risk-based

## Cost of Waiting

Intervening too early risks regulating uncertain technology; intervening too late allows harmful practices to become entrenched. DSA and DMA arrived when platform economy was already consolidated — agentic commerce has not reached that point, which is precisely why this is the moment to shape rules.
