# Autonomous AI Agents in Digital Markets: Economic Implications for Competition, Pricing, and Regulation

## Source

- **Authors:** Elmira Kydyrbayeva, Balhiya Shomshekhova, Asset Abzhakov, Ainur Ashimova, Assel Nurtayeva
- **Affiliations:** Zhetysu University (Kazakhstan), Academy of Public Administration under the President of the Republic of Kazakhstan, Almaty Management University
- **Journal:** *Applied Computer Science* 2026, vol. 22, no. 2, pp. 126–137
- **DOI:** 10.35784/acs_9272
- **CC-BY 4.0**
- **Dates:** Submitted 2026-02-27 | Revised 2026-04-29 | Accepted 2026-05-05

## Summary

This research analyses the effects of market transparency and algorithmic autonomy on pricing behaviour, competition, and consumer welfare in digital markets where autonomous AI agents make pricing decisions. Using a controlled simulation model, the study compares four pricing regimes:

1. **Human-supervised:** Sellers use AI-assisted pricing with human oversight.
2. **Fully autonomous:** AI agents set prices independently.
3. **Isolated:** Autonomous agents operate without market data from competitors.
4. **Mediated:** Autonomous agents interact through a platform-mediated governance structure.

## Key Findings

- **Market transparency and platform oversight matter more than autonomy:** The degree of market transparency and the degree of platform oversight of AI decision-making have a far greater impact on market outcomes than the level of algorithmic autonomy itself.
- **Structural trade-offs are inherent:** There are unavoidable trade-offs between market efficiency, market stability, and competitive processes in markets where AI is used as an autonomous decision-making agent. Some configurations increase efficiency and profit while simultaneously increasing the risk of coordination failure, volatility, and concentration.
- **Autonomy ≠ collusion:** The findings challenge the narrative that "more autonomy = more collusion." Platform-mediated governance can be more effective than restricting autonomy alone.
- **Intent-based regulation has limited utility:** The results highlight the limitations of attempting to regulate AI through intent-based regulations — because coordinated behaviour can emerge without explicit human intent, focusing on intent is an ineffective regulatory approach.

## Regulatory Implications

This paper has direct implications for UK regulatory strategy:

- **Platform-level regulation over autonomy bans:** The CMA should focus on platform-level transparency requirements and governance structures rather than banning or restricting autonomous pricing outright.
- **Transparency as a primary lever:** Higher market transparency does not necessarily increase collusion risk — in some mediated configurations it can improve consumer welfare outcomes.
- **Monitoring structural trade-offs:** Regulators need tools to track the inherent trade-offs between efficiency, stability, and competition in AI-driven markets.
- **Beyond intent-based enforcement:** The CMA's competition enforcement should move toward outcome-based regulation rather than requiring proof of human intent for coordinated pricing.
