# Agentic AI and Consumer Protection

**CERRE Issue Paper, July 2026** — Christoph Busch (Professor of Law, University of Osnabrück; European Law Institute Fellow; EC Consumer Policy Advisory Group member)

## Overview

A foundational policy analysis asking whether EU consumer law is ready for agentic AI and how it should be adapted. Produced as part of CERRE's Regulatory Lab series after a May 2026 expert convening with corporate members (Apple, Booking.com, Microsoft), national regulators, EU institutions, and civil society.

## Key Findings

- **Market shift underway**: 46% of EU consumers already use AI tools to discover products; 63% use AI to compare offers. Forecasts suggest 10–20% of e-commerce could be agent-handled by 2030, with agentic commerce potentially orchestrating $3–5 trillion in global transactions.
- **EU consumer law is human-centric by design**: Every core concept — mandatory disclosures, design-based standards, consent requirements, withdrawal rights — presupposes a human consumer. AI agents fundamentally challenge this premise.
- **The information paradigm gap**: Pre-contractual disclosure requirements (long lists of standardised information) are designed for humans with cognitive limitations. Agents are *not* constrained by these limitations — they can process vastly more information and filter for what's relevant to the individual consumer's preferences. This could *mitigate* one of the biggest criticisms of the information paradigm.
- **Consent and the "explicit acknowledgement" problem**: Article 8(2) of the Consumer Rights Directive requires consumers to "explicitly acknowledge" a payment obligation (typically via a button). It remains unclear whether an AI agent can meaningfully perform this acknowledgement on the consumer's behalf, or whether a separate human confirmation step is still required.
- **GUI vs. protocol layer**: Most consumer law provisions presuppose a human clicking through a graphical user interface. As transactions increasingly move to API/protocol layers, prescriptive design rules (button text, font size, visual prominence) become obsolete. But the underlying substantive standards (informed consent, prohibition on material distortion of economic behaviour) remain critical.
- **Mitigation vs. exacerbation of vulnerabilities**: AI agents could reduce information asymmetries (accessing reviews, product tests, forums beyond any human's reach) but may also exacerbate other vulnerabilities — particularly around consent erosion, invisible spending, and the difficulty of reversing agent-executed transactions.
- **Personalised law for agents**: The paper explores the possibility of "personalised law" — rules that adapt to the specific context and profile of an AI agent acting on a consumer's behalf — but warns this could create new forms of discrimination and opacity.
- **Transition framework**: Proposes a phased approach to adapting consumer law, including interim guidance, regulatory sandboxes for agent-mediated commerce, and a review of the design of consumer rights in the post-GUI era.

## Consumer Harm Vectors Identified

1. **Consent erosion** — Agents executing transactions without meaningful human confirmation at each step
2. **Information overload reversal** — What was a human protection becomes an agent advantage (agents can parse disclosures humans can't)
3. **GUI-to-protocol migration** — Consumer protections designed for visual interfaces become unenforceable at the API layer
4. **Reversibility gap** — Agent-executed purchases harder to unwind than human-initiated ones
5. **Personalised law opacity** — Rules adapting to agent profiles could embed discriminatory practices invisible to consumers

## Regulatory Implications

The paper provides a structured analysis of which existing EU consumer law provisions will remain relevant and which will need redesign. Of particular significance for UK regulators: the UK Consumer Rights Act 2015 and Consumer Protection from Unfair Trading Regulations 2008 inherit the same human-centric assumptions as EU law, creating parallel readiness gaps post-Brexit. The analysis is directly relevant to [[the-mills-review-summary]]'s recommendations on agentic finance infrastructure.
