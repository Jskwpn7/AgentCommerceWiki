# Automated Compliance and the Regulation of AI — O'Keefe & Frazier (2026)

## Paper Details

- **Authors:** Cullen O'Keefe, Kevin Frazier
- **Published:** 2026-01-05 (Working Paper No. 1-2026)
- **Source:** [Institute for Law & AI](https://law-ai.org/automated-compliance-and-the-regulation-of-ai/)
- **SSRN:** [6017756](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=6017756)
- **PDF:** [Working Paper](https://law-ai.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/Automated-Compliance-WPS-PDF-1.pdf)
- **Reading time:** ~25 minutes
- **Tags:** Legal Frontiers, Corporate & Market Regulation, Good Governance & Institutions, Judiciary & Legal Profession

## Summary

This working paper introduces the concept of **automated compliance** — the idea that AI systems will increasingly be able to perform regulatory compliance tasks cheaply and autonomously. The authors argue this has significant implications for AI policy, particularly the timing and content of regulations targeting AI itself.

The central thesis is that automated compliance **loosens the traditional trade-off** between regulation and innovation. Under standard AI policy debate, regulators face a choice between regulating too soon (potentially stifling innovation and competitiveness) versus too late or too leniently (risking preventable harms). The paper argues that if AI can automate its own compliance, then the cost of early regulation declines as AI capability advances — the very systems being regulated become capable of bearing the compliance burden.

## Key Arguments

### 1. Regulatory Costs Create the Innovation-Regulation Trade-Off
Regulation imposes compliance costs on regulated parties, creating the familiar policy dilemma: stricter rules reduce risk but may chill innovation. This trade-off is especially salient in AI policy, where speed of development is seen as strategically important.

### 2. Automated Compliance as a Game-Changer
Future AI systems will be able to perform many compliance tasks autonomously — monitoring, reporting, impact assessments, documentation, and even adaptive regulatory navigation. This means the cost of compliance with AI regulations could **fall as AI capability increases**, rather than rising.

### 3. "Automatability Triggers" for Regulation
The paper proposes a novel regulatory mechanism: regulations that become effective only when AI systems are capable of largely automating compliance with them. This would:
- Reduce the risk of premature regulation harming nascent industries
- Mitigate concerns that regulations disproportionately benefit large incumbents
- Create a more dynamic regulatory posture aligned with technological progress

### 4. Compliance-Automating AI as Evidence of Compliance
Implementing automated compliance workflows could itself serve as evidence of regulatory compliance — demonstrating that an organization has invested in compliance infrastructure proportional to its risk profile.

### 5. Differential Acceleration and Equity Concerns
While automated compliance could benefit startups (lowering barriers), the paper notes that development of compliance-automating AI services may concentrate in large firms, potentially creating new barriers. Targeted policy support for compliance-automating AI as a service could help.

### 6. Automated Compliance Meets Automated Governance
The paper connects regulated-entity automation with regulator-side automation — suggesting that optimal outcomes require both sides of the regulatory relationship to adopt AI tools responsibly.

## Policy Implications (per the paper)

1. **AI progress will reduce the cost of certain regulatory compliance tasks.**
2. **Policymakers can reduce risks from premature regulation by using "automatability triggers"** to stipulate that regulations become effective only when AI tools are capable of automating compliance.
3. **Proper implementation of compliance-automating AI workflows may provide evidence of regulatory compliance.**
4. **Targeted measures can support the development of compliance-automating AI services.**
5. **Automated compliance is most effective when complemented by the responsible automation of regulator-side oversight tasks.**

## Relevance to UK Consumer Harms

While the paper focuses on AI regulation broadly, its arguments map onto UK consumer protection in several ways:

- **Compliance burden and market concentration:** UK consumer regulation (FCA, CMA, Ofcom) already faces criticism for disproportionately burdening smaller players. The automated compliance thesis suggests AI could lower these barriers — but only if compliance-automating tools are widely accessible.
- **Digital platforms and automated governance:** The paper's section on automated governance has direct implications for how UK regulators (CMA, ICO) could use AI to monitor compliance with consumer protection rules, data protection obligations, and platform governance requirements.
- **Cross-sector relevance:** The principles apply to financial services, telecoms, energy, and digital platforms — all domains covered by the wiki's consumer harms research.
- **Timing of regulation:** The UK's approach to AI governance (pro-innovation, sectoral) aligns with the paper's emphasis on dynamic, capability-aligned regulation rather than one-size-fits-all mandates.
