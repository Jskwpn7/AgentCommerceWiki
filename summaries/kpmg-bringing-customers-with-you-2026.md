# KPMG UK — Bringing customers with you

> Author: Paul Riseborough, Partner, Financial Services Advisory, KPMG UK
> Source: [KPMG UK](https://kpmg.com/uk/en/insights/ai/bringing-customers-with-you.html)
> Date: 2026 (references Mills Review July 2026)

KPMG UK's practical framework for customer-facing readiness in agentic banking, positioned as the complement to producer-side governance. The article argues that while banks focus on model risk and regulatory compliance, the customer-side conversation is "mostly absent" — and that risk lies not in customer rejection of AI but in customers forming trust relationships with competitors' AI instead.

## Key premise

**The customer readiness gap is where banks lose.** Over 28 million UK adults already use AI tools to manage money (Lloyds CDI 2025), but not through their bank. The Mills Review's Yonder survey (5,026 UK adults) found 55% see at least one benefit of AI for financial services, but only 20% would use AI that acts autonomously. The gap between openness in principle and comfort with delegation in practice is where banks must invest.

> "The banks that win the agentic decade will be those that make AI-driven outcomes comprehensible, contestable and reversible from the customer's point of view."

## Seven practical moves

### 1. Stickering
Persistent, plain-English visual markers on any AI-touched banking journey. Analogous to traffic-light food labelling. The test: any retail customer, in any channel, can tell at a glance which parts of their experience were AI-driven. Avoids "over-stickering" (nearly all credit decisions involve ML).

### 2. Signposting
Explicit, customer-facing declaration of an agent's scope. "Our AI assistant can do A, B, C. It can suggest D, but you'll need to confirm. It can't do E." Clear escalation paths engineered into the first interstitial screen. Customers underestimate simple tasks (budgeting) and overestimate complex ones (regulated advice).

### 3. Right to a human
Guaranteed, one-tap route from any agentic interaction to a named human channel, with full context handover. The agent's context — what the customer was doing, what was offered, where they got stuck — passes automatically. Human can override on the spot with clear audit trail. "The single fastest way to lose trust in an AI system is to make that human path harder than it needs to be."

### 4. Right to appeal
Explicit right to appeal any AI-driven decision, with published SLA, human reviewer commitment, and written explanation. Quarterly publication of aggregate appeal volumes and reversal rates. A 'challenge this decision' link on AI outcomes with guaranteed response time.

### 5. Agent Time summary (undo history)
Regular, plain-English digest of agent activity. Undo option on every line where unwinding is feasible. Analogous to Apple Screen Time or Google Your Activity. Opted-into by default. Also serves as evidence for Consumer Duty outcomes testing.

### 6. Setting the rules in advance
Customer pre-commitment "ground rules hub" — spending caps, subscription reviews, times the agent cannot act. Analogous to smart-thermostat presets or parental controls. Vulnerability characteristics become tighter default pre-commitments, not just status flags. Consumer Duty foreseeable-harm logic baked into defaults.

### 7. Upskilling the frontline
Bank-wide AI literacy programme for customer-facing staff: comprehension, override authority, vulnerability recognition. The Financial Services Skills Commission's Annual Skills Report 2026 found AI skills among the largest supply-demand gaps. Frontline must see what the agent did, explain it, and confidently override it.

## Consumer harm relevance

**Dark pattern risk via opacity:** Stickering directly addresses the opacity problem — customers cannot contest or unwind what they cannot see. This is a form of **choice-architecture** harm: AI-touched journeys indistinguishable from human ones create informational asymmetry.

**Vulnerability protection:** Move 6 (ground rules hub) is particularly notable for consumer protection. Making vulnerability characteristics into tighter default pre-commitments, rather than just status flags, operationalises the Consumer Duty's foreseeable-harm logic. This prevents agents from exploiting known vulnerabilities.

**Accountability gap:** Moves 3 and 4 directly address the Mills Review's accountability gap finding. Without visible human escalation paths and explicit appeal rights, AI-driven outcomes have worse recourse than human ones — because no one designed the appeals flow.

**Consent and autonomy:** Move 6 (pre-commitment ground rules) reframes consent from post-facto agreement to proactive boundary-setting. This aligns with the Mills Review's autonomy spectrum, where each step toward autonomous agents places greater weight on consent, accountability, and redress.

**Trust deficit:** The 20% autonomous-use figure from Yonder shows the depth of the trust gap. The article's thesis — that trust is the ceiling on agentic AI, not technical capability — has direct consumer protection implications. Banks that don't solve for trust cede the relationship to hyperscalers' AI.

## Actionable takeaway

The article positions customer-facing design (not technology) as the competitive differentiator. "Banks that get pre-commitment design right within the next 12 months will have something hyperscalers do not: customer-set boundaries for a regulated environment."

The 90-day retrofit of stickering/signposting onto existing AI-touched journeys is a concrete near-term action for regulated firms.
