# The Mills Review: AI and the Future of Retail Financial Services

## Foreword

AI is already part of retail financial services. Used well, it can help firms serve customers more effectively, detect harm earlier and support better financial decisions. But as the technology becomes more capable and more widely used, it may also change how consumers engage with financial services, how firms compete and how markets operate.

The FCA Board asked Sheldon Mills to conduct this Review into how advances in AI could transform retail financial services by 2030 and beyond. They drew on 140 written submissions and meetings, panels and roundtables across financial services, technology, academia, consumer groups and regulation in the UK and abroad.

Key quote: "Son, that AI is not going away now, you cannot switch it off, can you?" — the question is no longer whether to allow AI, it is who AI is going to serve.

The pace has been the surprise: more than 20 frontier models have been released since Mills started in late 2025. Firms are moving from systems that recommend actions to systems empowered to take them. As autonomy grows, risks shift from harm within a single firm towards system-wide harms.

Mills argues for an "Agentic Supervisory Model" giving the FCA AI-enabled tools to monitor and detect emerging system-wide risks. "Striking the balance between enabling delegation and managing autonomy is the central challenge."

## Executive Summary

AI will transform retail financial services by 2030 and beyond. The central shift is from human-led, episodic financial activity towards services that are AI-enabled, continuous and delegated. AI will operate inside firms, through consumer interfaces, across markets and within regulators.

### Consumer Benefits
AI can address longstanding weaknesses: the advice gap (only 9% of consumers use traditional advice), the protection gap (just 30% hold life or income protection), low switching, financial exclusion (around 900,000 unbanked) and suboptimal saving (£300bn in low-interest accounts).

### Consumer Research Findings
- 1 in 5 UK adults already open to AI making decisions for them (strongest for debt advice, pensions, investments)
- ~26% trust general-purpose tools (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini) for financial advice, despite limited awareness that formal recourse routes will not apply
- Trust, control and access are what must be right before consumers adopt AI finance agents

### Four Systemic Shifts
1. **AI transforms firms** — embedding AI into almost every function, moving from recommendations to autonomous actions
2. **Consumer journeys become agent-led** — people increasingly delegate to AI applications
3. **AI reshapes market power and competition** — control of AI-mediated customer interface becomes a major source of market power
4. **Threats and defences both accelerate** — AI amplifies fraud and cyber risks

### Regulatory Framework Assessment
The overall regulatory framework remains sound. The principles and outcomes-based approach, including the Consumer Duty and Senior Managers Regime, was designed to flex across changing business models. But firms and regulators will need to work through how the SMR operates as AI systems become more autonomous.

### Systemic Risk
Shared reliance on similar models, datasets and infrastructure providers could generate correlated behaviour, herding, opacity and common points of failure. At market structure level, dependence on a few upstream technology providers could affect competition, resilience, sovereignty and smaller firms' access to advanced capability.

### Seven Priority Recommendations
1. Secure and adapt the regulatory perimeter
2. Strengthen system-wide coordination and oversight
3. Monitor the transition to autonomous models and adapt regulatory frameworks
4. Scale up the FCA's AI Lab
5. Enable the foundations for agentic finance
6. Build and adopt an AI-enabled agentic supervisory model
7. Develop a trusted public-interest AI-enabled financial capability service

## The AI Autonomy Spectrum

Five stages along which humans move from performing tasks towards setting boundaries and overseeing outcomes:

1. **Operator** — human uses AI as a tool to understand, analyse or complete tasks. Risks: accuracy, evidence, appropriate reliance.
2. **Collaborator** — human and AI plan and act together. Risks: advice-like influence, over-reliance, weak challenge.
3. **Consultant** — AI compares options and recommends; human decides. Risks: same as collaborator plus advice regulation questions.
4. **Approver** — AI prepares or initiates actions; human authorises key steps. Risks: reliance on permissions and safeguards set in advance.
5. **Observer** — AI acts continuously within boundaries; human monitors outcomes. Risks: consent, accountability, auditability, redress.

The further along the spectrum, the more accountability becomes harder to trace, especially where models change over time and firms depend on external providers.

## System Shift 1: The Transformation of Firms

Retail financial services firms deploying AI for specific tasks, mostly keeping humans in the loop. By 2030, many could have moved significantly further along the autonomy spectrum. For leading firms, AI may become the main method for processing information, serving customers, and evidencing outcomes.

As autonomy grows, the role of people within firms changes from operators close to each decision, towards collaborators, approvers and observers who monitor outcomes. Firm governance will extend existing model risk management to cover more complex systems and deeper reliance on third-party providers.

## System Shift 2: New Consumer Journeys

AI will reshape consumer financial journeys, with people increasingly delegating to AI applications that act on their behalf. Over time, AI systems will move beyond offering information and recommendations towards trusted AI agents that can act continuously for consumers within agreed limits.

Hyper-personalisation could help better match products to needs, but also enable bias, opaque pricing and personalised manipulation. Access to useful and reliable AI services, trust and control will define consumer uptake. Unequal access to high-quality applications risks widening inclusion gaps.

## System Shift 3: A Reshaped Competition Landscape

AI has the potential to drive greater beneficial competition, yet is also likely to create significant dependencies on suppliers who hold market power over firms' core operational capability. Control of the AI-mediated customer interface may become a major source of market power. As consumers rely on agents to search, compare and transact, the owner of that AI layer may influence which products are visible, how choices are ranked and where value is captured.

This control could take several forms: operating-system assistants, general-purpose apps, aggregators and consumers' own agents. Much depends on upstream technology markets — competition among model providers, big tech and hyperscalers governs access to frontier capability, compute and data. Cost, concentration, vendor lock-in and sovereignty could all become critical.

Under an activity-based approach, general-purpose tools could shape financial decisions and competition without clear oversight. Whether this influence falls inside or outside the perimeter will shape how competition and consumer protection work in practice.

## System Shift 4: Amplified Financial Crime and Cyber Risk

By 2030, AI is likely to amplify fraud and cyber risks, making attacks faster, cheaper, more scalable and more persuasive — and at the same time harder to spot and stop. Deepfakes, synthetic identities and personalised social engineering are taking fraud and cyber risks into a new era.

As consumers increasingly rely on AI agents to manage financial decisions and transactions, risk will spread more quickly across an increasingly interconnected model-based system. Defenders will need access to many of the same AI capabilities as those used by attackers.

## Regulatory Implications

### Accountability
No firm argued that the SMR accountability model should change, but many are considering how they continue to comply as automation increases. Firms will need to demonstrate they understand AI decisions, can demonstrate good outcomes, and maintain clear lines of accountability.

### Regulatory Perimeter
The perimeter was not designed to address two issues relevant to AI:
1. General-purpose AI applications shaping consumer financial decisions outside the perimeter
2. Financial services activity potentially becoming established outside the perimeter before oversight adapts

Mills recommends HM Treasury conduct a review of the regulatory perimeter, examining how activities involving material reliance on general-purpose LLMs should be treated. The FCA should work with HM Treasury to strengthen coordination on perimeter powers.

### Operational Resilience
The operational resilience framework establishes firm-level accountability for resilience. AI increases the importance of mapping technology supply chains and understanding dependencies on third-party providers.

### System-Wide Risk
AI creates a broader challenge beyond traditional prudential risk. Shared reliance on similar models, datasets and infrastructure providers could generate correlated behaviour, herding, opacity and common points of failure. If trust frameworks were adopted and enough consumers delegated financial activity to AI agents, continuous movement of money could occur as those agents optimise for individual customers — potentially changing pricing, products or services in markets such as savings and insurance.

### Agentic Supervisory Model
Supervision must extend beyond individual regulated firms to the financial ecosystem as a whole. Regulators will need AI-enabled supervisory capability to identify cross-firm patterns, emerging harms and system-wide risks.

## Priority Recommendations in Detail

### Recommendation 1: Secure and adapt the regulatory perimeter
Review the regulatory perimeter for AI-mediated retail financial services. Examine whether activities involving material reliance on general-purpose LLMs fall outside the perimeter. Strengthen coordination with HM Treasury on perimeter powers. Monitor AI developments in other sectors that may impact financial services.

### Recommendation 2: Strengthen system-wide coordination and oversight
Establish cross-regulator coordination on AI-driven systemic risk. Work with Bank of England, PRA, CMA, ICO and Ofcom. Build shared intelligence on AI developments and their financial services implications.

### Recommendation 3: Monitor the transition to autonomous models and adapt regulatory frameworks
Clarify how accountability, governance and consumer protection apply at each stage of the autonomy spectrum. Provide guidance on operating at higher autonomy levels. Update accountability frameworks for distributed decision-making.

### Recommendation 4: Scale up the FCA's AI Lab
Support AI model and system innovation in financial services. Use the AI Lab to test, learn and share best practice.

### Recommendation 5: Enable the foundations for agentic finance
Establish clear frameworks for consent, identity, authority, liability and execution to underpin safe agentic finance. Build an ecosystem of trusted protocols enabling AI systems to act on behalf of consumers securely.

The six building blocks identified:
1. **Data access** — open finance as the observation data layer
2. **Identity** — trusted, verifiable digital identity for consumers and agents
3. **Authority to act** — clear mandates, permissions and consent frameworks
4. **Execution** — secure, interoperable payment and transaction infrastructure
5. **Liability attribution** — clear chains of accountability when AI agents cause harm
6. **Supervision and audit at scale** — understanding what agents did, on what authority, and with what result

### Recommendation 6: Build and adopt an AI-enabled agentic supervisory model
Develop AI-enabled tools for the FCA to monitor the financial ecosystem holistically. Identify cross-firm patterns, emerging harms and system-wide risks. Support and enhance human supervision so AI opportunity is not outweighed by risks of harm.

### Recommendation 7: Develop a trusted public-interest AI-enabled financial capability service
Provide consumers with free, fair and impartial AI-powered financial guidance. Address the trust gap where consumers use general-purpose AI tools for financial advice despite limited awareness of absence of recourse. Must be clearly independent from commercial interests.

## Consumer Research

### Survey Findings (Annex III)
- 40% of consumers comfortable with AI making simple financial decisions
- Only 40% understand who is accountable if an AI makes a bad financial recommendation
- 32% cite "clear protections if something goes wrong" as most important adoption driver
- 30% cite "evidence of accuracy and reliability" as most important adoption driver
- Concern about over-trusting AI outputs, particularly for high-stakes decisions
- Consumer understanding of accountability and recourse is limited

### Focus Group Findings (Annex IV)
- Consumers frequently raise concerns around accuracy, reliability and data use
- Many remain reluctant to relinquish control over financial decisions
- Tension between convenience and control is the dominant theme
- Trust in existing institutions is variable; trust in AI tools is even more variable

## Key Consumer Harm Risks Identified

1. **Unprotected reliance** — consumers trust AI outputs with no accountability or recourse chain
2. **Hyper-personalisation** — could enable bias, opaque pricing and personalised manipulation
3. **Accountability gaps** — difficulty tracing responsibility when AI agents act autonomously
4. **Market power concentration** — control of AI-mediated interfaces becomes source of market power
5. **Financial exclusion** — unequal access to high-quality AI tools widens inclusion gaps
6. **Correlated systemic risk** — shared models and infrastructure creating common failure points
7. **Regulatory perimeter gaps** — general-purpose AI shaping financial decisions without oversight
8. **Amplified fraud** — AI enables faster, more scalable, more persuasive attacks
