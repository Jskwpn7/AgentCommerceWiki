# This Time It's Personal: The Rise of Dynamic, Personalised Pricing and What It Means for Inflation

**Bank of England Insights** | 2026
**Authors:** Clare Lombardelli, Rupal Patel

---

## The Rise of Algorithmic Pricing

Technology is enabling this shift. Digitalisation has radically reduced what economists call menu costs – the expense of changing listed prices – so called historically because of the costs associated with having new menus printed with updated prices. Digital pricing allows firms to change prices frequently at negligible cost.

Online travel and hotel platforms, once simply passive listing sites, now dynamically alter their prices as demand ebbs and flows.

**Key stat:** The proportion of hotel room rates that change at least once per month has risen from around **15% in 2005** to roughly **80% today**.

Technology has also allowed businesses to collect, process and store more data on consumers and their shopping preferences. Machine learning models are enabling more finely tuned price setting using this data. Instead of relying on simple rules like peak and off-peak fares, firms increasingly deploy predictive models that infer demand curves and monitor competitors to optimise their own prices.

In many cases, consumers now see price promotions unique to them or to people with characteristics or shopping habits similar to them: loyalty card discounts, customised bundles or subtle nudges to steer them toward higher margin products. The result is different customers paying different prices for the same or very similar goods and services.

Through these practices firms are seeking to move towards the textbook view of **"perfect price discrimination"** – charging as close to the maximum price a consumer is willing to pay for a good or service.

## How Much Dynamic and Personalised Pricing Is Going On?

The sophistication of such technology-enabled pricing varies across the economy. Firms keep their pricing strategies a closely guarded secret. The Bank's **Decision Maker Panel (DMP) Survey** (three months to January 2026) asked over **1,600 firms** about their use of:

### Three Tiers

**Rules-based pricing** – prices that follow simple pre-set rules or vary by time of day, week or season (e.g., peak/off-peak train tickets) or automatic adjustments when stock levels change.

**Market-responsive pricing** – where firms use algorithms, including AI, to adjust prices in response to data on demand, capacity or competitors' prices.

**Personalised pricing** – prices that differ across customers based on characteristics or behaviour. This could be simple strategies such as loyalty schemes or more sophisticated algorithms applied to data on individual or group shopping habits.

### DMP Survey Findings (Chart 2)

- All sectors use these strategies to different degrees
- All sectors are proposing to **increase** their use over the coming year
- Largest planned increase: **market-responsive pricing** from 21% currently to 31% in a year
- Personalised pricing is "widely adopted across all sectors" — largely still simple forms (loyalty cards, online customer accounts)
- Electronic shelf labels in supermarkets (widespread in Europe) being experimented with in the UK
- Some theme parks now use algorithms with actual real-time capacity data rather than weekday/weekend pricing
- Simple rule-based dynamic pricing is becoming "outdated and replaced by market-responsive dynamic pricing using algorithms"

### Adoption Constraints

Adoption in the UK may be limited more by **reputational considerations** than by technological ones:
- Businesses worry about consumer backlash from opaque/unfair price changes
- **UK consumers are more likely than consumers in other countries to consider dynamic pricing unfair** (YouGov 2024)
- Businesses may be wary of regulatory actions

## Challenging Inflation Measurement

A world of more fluid and bespoke prices makes life harder for statisticians, while the power of data analysis may make it easier.

### The CPI Measurement Problem

The consumer prices index (CPI) relies on collecting a representative sample of prices for a standardised basket **once a month**. That works well when prices move slowly and uniformly. But when prices shift continually – and differently for each shopper – the idea of a "representative price" becomes strained.

**Price volatility:** Hotel and airfare prices, now highly dynamic, increasingly inject noise into the monthly inflation reading. As firms change prices more frequently, month-to-month inflation becomes harder to measure and interpret. The greater flexibility also means shocks may pass through more quickly to prices, meaning some jagged movements may actually be informative.

**Central bank response:** Bank of England excludes hotel and airfares prices (where prices are set dynamically) from CPI "core services" measure. More broadly, central banks have expanded analytical capability to extract signals from many different data sources.

### The Personalised Pricing Splinter Problem

The rise of personalised pricing splinters the consumer experience and raises the question of what it really means for a price to be "representative".

- Households already face different inflation rates because they buy different things in different ways
- Inflation rates experienced by different **income deciles vary** because of different consumption baskets
- These different consumption baskets can become **more segmented** with personalised pricing
- When prices differ for the same thing, inflation becomes even more personalised – and **aggregate measures may no longer reflect households' experience**

### ONS Scanner Data — A "Small Revolution" (Chart 3)

In **March 2026**, ONS started to use bulk weekly grocery scanner data:
- Uses far more prices in the CPI measure
- Collected over a greater time period
- For the first time capturing **loyalty card prices** and **person-specific discounts**
- Covers retailers representing around **half of the grocery market**

**Impact on CPI:** Very small overall. Early analysis estimates:
- Scanner data would have **reduced** headline CPI inflation by **0.03pp** from Jan 2022 to Jun 2025
- **0.1pp higher** during months when food price inflation was particularly elevated
- For individual goods/services, differences could be larger
- "These relatively small overall effects will continue to mask much larger differences between the prices faced by different households purely because they consume different baskets"

ONS has published quarterly Household Cost Indices (since 2023) showing prices paid by different types of households.

## Will Dynamic Pricing Raise Inflation?

For now, algorithmic pricing does not appear to be an **inflationary menace** and is used more to manage capacity than raise prices.

**Key null result:** Despite the explosion in dynamic pricing since 2005, the cumulative increase in accommodation prices is **broadly in line** with the prices of less dynamic services.

Dynamic pricing and greater price discrimination might:
- Lead to prices being higher than normal for some consumers
- Lead to prices being lower than normal for other consumers
- Enable consumers who previously could not afford certain goods to access them
- Allow better use of spare capacity and increased overall economic activity
- Lower costs through better matching of supply and demand (e.g., surge pricing for airlines and ride-hailing)

For these strategies to benefit consumers, there need to be "fit-for-purpose safeguards to ensure consistency both with good ethical standards and the law."

CMA have issued guidance on price transparency, with "clear legal and reputational implications."

How dynamic and personalised pricing will affect inflation depends on:
- Degree of competition in a market
- Availability of information about prices to consumers
- How it affects formation of households' inflation expectations

## Information and Competition

Data advances give firms unprecedented insight into what customers are willing to pay. In theory, this allows them to price-discriminate better and capture more **consumer surplus**.

- In markets with **limited competition**, mark-ups could rise, and with them, prices
- Where **rivalry is fierce**, better information on customers can lead firms to undercut competitors, keeping a lid on prices
- Customers increasingly have access to better information on firms offering products, increasing consumers' price elasticity of demand, limiting firms' ability to raise prices
- AI shopping assistants help people hunt for bargains, though benefits accrue mostly to digitally savvy consumers

### Algorithmic Collusion Acknowledgment

> "In some unsettling experiments, algorithms have learnt over time to charge above competitive and static prices and drifted into tacit, collusive behaviour after discovering that constant undercutting is bad for profits."

- Algorithmic collusion is still "rare, but regulators are alert"
- CMA, FTC, EU all monitoring this space

## Inflation Expectations — The Psychological Channel

Households notice price rises more than price falls, and form inflation expectations on prices of goods and services they purchase frequently (food and fuel).

**If dynamic pricing becomes more common in those categories, more volatile prices may cause perceptions of inflation to drift upwards – even if average prices do not.**

### Bank's Inflation Attitudes Survey

When prices fluctuate because of AI-enabled dynamic pricing, people **perceive that prices are higher on average**. This may lead to them forming higher inflation expectations in the future.

**44%** of households expected that the prices they would personally pay would be **higher** as a result of firms using technology to dynamically set prices.
**29%** expect prices about the same.
**8%** expect prices lower.
Remaining respondents were unsure.

### Chart 4 — Price Change Frequency vs Impact on Inflation Expectations

Dynamic pricing is somewhat limited in sectors that have the most bearing on forming inflation expectations. **Food is an outlier** — it has a high degree of dynamic pricing AND high effect on people's expectations of inflation.

**Key risk:** As these pricing strategies expand into food and other high-impact categories, "this could lead to an upward ratchet on inflation expectations as food prices become more volatile and consumers notice the increases more than the decreases."

### Transparency as a Solution

If consumers understand that price fluctuations reflect demand conditions and that higher prices are short-lived, expectations can remain anchored. Example: rail travel peak/off-peak pricing is "well communicated and widely understood." Transparency can reduce price uncertainty and keep expectations anchored when prices fluctuate.

---

## Conclusion (From the Article)

Dynamic and personalised pricing have not so far radically altered the overall inflation process. But they are changing it, and the direction of travel is clear. As data-driven pricing, particularly driven by new technologies such as AI, becomes more prevalent:

- Prices will move faster and vary more across consumers
- This creates new opportunities for both firms and consumers
- Central banks need to keep track of how this affects consumer behaviour and inflation
- Central banks need to continue improving tools for extracting information about underlying economic conditions from more volatile and more fragmented prices
- More understanding needed of how households and firms experience inflation and what this means for their inflation expectations
